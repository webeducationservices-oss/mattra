Leaking Water Heater or Appliance? What to Do Next

By Mattra Inc. __PUBLISH_DATE_LONG__ 7 min read

Plenty of water damage starts inside the house: the water heater in the basement, the washing machine hose, the line behind the refrigerator, or a drip under the kitchen sink that's been going for months. Some of these fail all at once. Others leak slowly enough that nobody notices until the floor goes soft.

Here's what to do when an appliance or plumbing line leaks, what each one usually looks like when it fails, and how to keep the damage small. When the water has already spread, Mattra handles water damage cleanup and restoration for homes in Lewiston-Auburn and across Maine.

First Steps for Any Appliance Leak

  1. Shut off the water. Most appliances have their own supply valve behind or under them. If you can't find it or it won't turn, shut off the main.
  2. Shut off the power or gas. Unplug the washer or dishwasher. For an electric water heater, turn off its breaker. For a gas water heater, turn the gas control to off.
  3. Get the water up. Towels and a wet-vac for small amounts. Move anything on the floor out of the way.
  4. Photograph the leak and the damage before anything is repaired or thrown away.
  5. Call a plumber or appliance tech for the source, and call for help with the water damage if it reached walls, cabinets, or the floor below.

Water Heater Leaks

Water pooling around the base of a water heater is the one to take seriously. If the tank itself is leaking from the bottom, that usually means it has corroded through, and the tank needs to be replaced before it lets go completely. Leaks from the pipe fittings on top or from the temperature and pressure relief valve can often be repaired.

A drain pan under the tank, piped to a floor drain, catches a slow leak before it spreads across the basement. Check the manufacture date on the label. An older tank sitting on a finished basement floor is worth replacing before it fails.

Washing Machine Leaks

Supply hoses are the most common failure, and when one bursts it can release a large amount of water very quickly. Many appliance makers recommend replacing rubber supply hoses every five years. Braided stainless hoses hold up better. A drain hose that slips out of the standpipe floods the laundry room just as fast.

It's a good habit to avoid running the washer when nobody's home.

Dishwasher Leaks

Dishwashers usually leak from a worn door gasket, a loose supply line, or the drain hose. The trouble is that much of that water ends up under the dishwasher and under the cabinets, where it soaks the subfloor without showing on the kitchen floor. Swelling at the edge of nearby cabinets or a soft spot in front of the dishwasher are the signs.

Refrigerator Water Line Leaks

The thin plastic line that feeds an ice maker or water dispenser can crack, especially after the fridge gets pulled out and pushed back. Since it's hidden behind the refrigerator, a slow drip can run for weeks. Look for warped flooring near the fridge or water stains on the ceiling below the kitchen.

Toilet and Under-Sink Leaks

  • Toilet supply lines can burst like washer hoses.
  • A failed wax ring lets water seep out at the base every time the toilet is flushed. The floor around it gets soft, and the subfloor underneath slowly rots.
  • Under-sink leaks from the drain trap, supply lines, or faucet swell the cabinet floor and feed mold in the back of the cabinet.

Why Slow Leaks Do More Damage Than They Look Like

A sudden burst is obvious and gets handled the same day. A slow leak keeps the same patch of wood wet for weeks or months, which is exactly what mold and rot need.

  • Rot in subfloor, joists, and sill plates. See rot repair.
  • Mold inside cabinets, behind walls, and under flooring. See mold removal and remediation.
  • Ceiling damage in the room below a bathroom or kitchen.

Watch for:

  • A musty smell in a bathroom, kitchen, or laundry room
  • Soft, spongy, or cupped flooring
  • Stains on the ceiling under a bathroom or kitchen
  • A water bill that jumps for no reason
  • On a well, a pressure pump that kicks on when nobody is using water

How to Prevent Appliance Leaks

  • Replace old rubber supply hoses on washers and toilets.
  • Put a drain pan under the water heater and washing machine.
  • Set inexpensive water leak alarms on the floor by the water heater, washer, dishwasher, and fridge.
  • Consider an automatic shutoff valve that closes the main when it senses a leak.
  • Look under sinks and behind appliances a couple of times a year.

Does Insurance Cover Appliance Leaks?

Most homeowners policies cover sudden water damage, like a burst washer hose. Damage from a leak that went on for weeks or months is often excluded, and the broken appliance itself usually isn't covered. Common Questions About Appliance and Plumbing Leaks

Is a leaking water heater dangerous?

A tank leaking from the bottom is close to failing and can release its whole volume. Shut off the water and the power or gas to it and have it replaced soon.

Can a slow leak cause mold?

Yes. Mold needs moisture that stays, and a slow leak keeps wood and drywall wet for weeks. Mold behind cabinets and under floors from slow leaks is very common.
